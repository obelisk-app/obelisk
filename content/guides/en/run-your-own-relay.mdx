## An ordinary relay is a dumb store

It takes what you send it and hands it back. That is the whole contract, and for
most of Nostr it is the right one.

It does mean a relay has no opinion about membership. Whoever holds a group id
can write to it, and "who is in this group" is whatever each client decides to
believe. A private group is private for exactly as long as every client agrees
to pretend that it is — which is not a security property, it is an etiquette.

## A NIP-29 relay decides instead

<Mark name="mark-relay" /> **obelisk-relay** enforces roles, membership and
visibility where the data actually lives, and filters on read. A non-member
asking for a private group's messages does not get a greyed-out button. They get
nothing, because there is nothing to send them.

That is a real improvement and a real burden. Deciding who may connect is now a
job somebody has to do, on a disk somebody has to pay for. Most of this article
is about the tools for that job.

## Two commands

<Clip name="relay/install" caption="Installation, end to end." />

```bash
git clone https://github.com/obelisk-app/obelisk-relay.git
cd obelisk-relay

./setup.sh     # a working relay on localhost:8080
./expose.sh    # publish it at wss://your.domain, when you're ready
```

The split is deliberate: each script has one job. `setup.sh` checks Docker, port
8080 and disk, asks for the npub that will administer the relay, writes a fresh
config and brings the container up. `expose.sh` walks you through a Cloudflare
Tunnel, writes a `cloudflared` sidecar, updates the advertised address and
restarts both together on reboot.

Stop after the first one and you have a relay on your laptop. That is a
legitimate place to stop.

<Callout type="warn" title="Pin the image tag">
`setup.sh` pulls `:latest`, which currently lags the dated builds — `:latest` is
multi-arch and the current builds are arm64-only, so moving it would break every
amd64 install. Until both architectures are published and joined, pass the tag
you want explicitly: `RELAY_IMAGE_TAG=<dated-tag> ./setup.sh`. Take the tag from
`compose.yml` in the repo rather than from the README, which has drifted behind
it before.
</Callout>

## First boot

With no `relay_secret_key` configured, the relay mints its own keypair on start
and writes it to `config/settings.local.yml`, logging the pubkey once. Keep
`config/` on a persistent volume: the relay's identity is what its group state
is anchored to, so a relay whose pubkey changed on every boot would invalidate
that state each time.

With no admin pubkeys configured it comes up in setup mode, and `/admin` shows a
wizard rather than a login screen. Three steps, all in the browser.

<Shot name="relay/sign-in" caption="You prove ownership by signing — there is no npub field to type into." maxWidth={420} />

Whichever key signs here becomes the first admin. That is worth reading twice:
you do not nominate an npub, you demonstrate control of one. Browser extension,
remote signer, or a pasted key if you insist. Then you choose whether the relay
is open or restricted, and it writes its config and restarts into the console.

<Callout type="note" title="The screenshots below are purple">
They are captures of the public relay at `wss://public.obelisk.ar`, which runs
the purple build. Obelisk's lime is the chat client. Same software, different
accent — your own relay is branded from the Settings screen.
</Callout>

## Relay health

<Shot name="relay/overview" caption="Overview: connections and disk over the last day, and one line for who is actually here." />

Connections and database size are drawn hourly and extended live while the
screen is open. The line worth understanding is **Disk used**, because it does
not behave the way a file usually does: the relay stores events in LMDB, which
reuses freed pages internally and never returns them to the filesystem. Deleting
events flattens that line rather than lowering it. A flat event count beside a
rising line means the database wants compacting, not pruning.

## Admission is a ladder, not a switch

This is the idea the whole product is built around, so it is worth stating
plainly before looking at any screen.

<Clip name="relay/ladder" caption="Three tiers and a block list, decided in that order." />

Someone you added by hand. Someone your reference accounts follow. Or someone
close enough in the follow graph to be worth the benefit of the doubt. Decided
in that order — and a block list that overrides all three, checked first.

<Shot name="relay/access-tabs" caption="One screen, five tabs, each with a live count." />

The **reference accounts** at the top of that screen are the roots of the whole
structure. They are the accounts every tier is measured from: everyone they
follow lands in Tier 1, and the follow graph is walked outward from there to
fill Tier 2 and Tier 3. Adding one reference account widens all three tiers at
once, which is the single highest-leverage control on the relay and the one to
think hardest about.

<Clip name="relay/numbers" caption="Two reference accounts, measured on public.obelisk.ar on 22 September 2026." />

Tier 2 is two hops out. Tier 3 is three, and the console says *at least* rather
than printing a total, because the outermost hop is the one the graph runs out
of budget to finish. Honest beats tidy — a number presented as complete when it
is not is worse than a floor you can reason about.

<Callout type="note" title="Those counts are a measurement, not a spec">
1,062 / 45,966 / ≥119,708 are what two reference accounts reached on one relay on
one day. They tell you the *shape* of the thing — that one account admits tens
of thousands of people two hops out — not a number your relay will reproduce.
Yours depends entirely on whose follow graph you rooted it in.
</Callout>

## What a tier costs you

Trust is not binary, so the budget is not either.

<Shot name="relay/budget-ladder" caption="Each tier publishes on a share of the per-pubkey budget." />

Tier 1 publishes at 6,000 events a minute. Tier 2 gets half of that, Tier 3 a
quarter, and anyone admitted while enforcement is off gets 600. The reasoning is
the same one that orders the ladder: the further from your reference accounts
someone is, the less evidence there is that they should be trusted with your
disk.

Two other limits sit above the per-pubkey one, and both matter more than they
look. The **per-connection** cap should sit above the per-pubkey budget — it
caps a single socket, and setting it lower makes the per-pubkey number
unreachable. The **whole-relay** cap is the backstop against a flood from
everybody at once, and it is the one that saves you when the first two were
tuned optimistically.

## Paste a key, get an answer

<Shot name="relay/key-search" caption="An npub, checked: allowed, admitted by web of trust, two hops out." />

Most access questions arrive as "why can't this person connect?", and this is
the box that answers them. It takes an npub, a hex key or a NIP-05 address, and
replies with the tier that admits them and how far away they are — or why they
were refused. The Block button sits beside the verdict, because the answer and
the action belong in the same place.

## Moderation that survives contact with reality

Ten people reporting one message is one decision, not ten. The queue groups by
what was reported, one row per thing, counting distinct reporters rather than
volume.

<Clip name="relay/snapshot" caption="The reported message, still readable after its author deleted it." />

The part that matters most is the least visible: **the relay snapshots the
reported message when the report lands.** Without that, a reported account
deletes their own message and the complaint becomes unreviewable — deletion as a
defence rather than a remedy.

<Shot name="relay/reports" caption="One row per reported thing, with the decision you made." />

Reports are readable by relay admins only, and that is a deliberate choice
rather than an oversight. On a relay where admission is a social graph, a public
report queue is a retaliation channel.

<Callout type="note" title="Reports filed before the snapshot landed">
Rows from before that change say so explicitly — "this was reported before the
relay began keeping a copy, and the original is gone". A moderation tool that
quietly shows you nothing is worse than one that tells you why it has nothing.
</Callout>

## Nothing expires unless you arm it

<Shot name="relay/storage" caption="Storage: what is on disk, and whether anything is being deleted." />

Automatic pruning is off until you turn it on, and retention is set **per event
kind**, so short-lived traffic can expire without touching conversations. Group
management and state kinds are never deleted, and neither is anything
replaceable or addressable — the relay keeps only one of those per user, so
deleting them would destroy live state and free no space worth having.

Count before you arm a policy. The overview samples rather than scanning, and the
sample over-represents gift wraps.

## Putting it behind a domain

The relay serves HTTP, WebSocket and its own frontend on one port. Bind it to
loopback and terminate TLS in front of it:

```
relay.example.com {
    reverse_proxy 127.0.0.1:8080
}
```

Caddy needs nothing special for WebSocket upgrades; nginx needs `Upgrade` and
`Connection` passed through.

<Callout type="warn" title="`relay_url` is not cosmetic">
It must match the public address clients actually reach you on. It is used for
NIP-42 authentication, so a stale value does not degrade gracefully — it breaks
auth outright. Set it from Settings → Relay identity after you have a domain.
</Callout>

## Pointing Obelisk at it

<Shot name="relay/landing" caption="Your relay serves its own front page, with the address ready to copy." />

Nothing about your relay is Obelisk-specific — it speaks NIP-29, and any client
that does will work. But if you want the chat client:

```
https://obelisk.ar/app?relay=relay.example.com
```

For something you can hand to a person rather than paste into a config, Obelisk
encodes a relay into a short share link at `obelisk.ar/r/<code>`. Visiting it
adds the relay to the reader's list and switches to it, and re-visiting is a
no-op — so it is safe to put in a pinned message or a bio.

The relay is [AGPL-3.0 on GitHub](https://github.com/obelisk-app/obelisk-relay),
and there is one running right now at `wss://public.obelisk.ar` if you would
rather look before you install.
